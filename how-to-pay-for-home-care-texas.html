How to Pay for Home Care in Texas

Domira Home Care · August 2026 · North Texas

Most families start looking into home care during a stressful stretch. A parent has had a fall, a hospital stay ended sooner than expected, or the everyday tasks have quietly gotten harder to manage alone. Almost immediately, a second question follows the first one: how do we actually pay for this?

The short answer is that non-medical home care in Texas is usually paid for privately, but private pay is not the only route, and many families qualify for help without realizing it. Below is a plain explanation of each option worth checking, what it actually covers, and how to find out where you stand.

First, Know Which Kind of Care You Are Asking About

This distinction determines everything else on this page, and it is where most of the confusion starts.

Non-medical home care is help with daily life: bathing, dressing, mobility, meals, light housekeeping, companionship, medication reminders, and supervision. It is provided by trained caregivers rather than nurses, and it is what Domira Home Care provides.

Home health care is clinical care ordered by a physician: skilled nursing, wound care, physical or occupational therapy. It is delivered by licensed clinicians and billed differently.

Insurance programs treat these two things very differently. When a program says it covers "home health," that almost never means it will pay for a caregiver to help your mother shower. For a fuller breakdown, see our guide on home care versus home health care.

Does Medicare Pay for Home Care?

For non-medical home care, no. This is the single most common misunderstanding families run into.

Medicare covers intermittent skilled home health when a physician certifies that it is medically necessary and the person is considered homebound. That means visits from a nurse or therapist for a defined clinical purpose, over a limited period.

Medicare does not pay for ongoing help with bathing, dressing, meals, housekeeping, or companionship, and it does not pay for a caregiver to be present for a shift. If someone tells you Medicare will cover a few hours of personal care each day, that is not accurate.

Some Medicare Advantage plans have begun offering limited supplemental in-home support benefits, and these vary widely by plan and by year. If your loved one is on an Advantage plan, it is worth calling the number on the back of the card and asking specifically whether the plan includes any in-home personal care or supportive services benefit. Most do not. A few do.

Private Pay

This is how the majority of non-medical home care in Texas is paid for, and it is the most flexible option. There is no eligibility process, no waiting list, and no restriction on what the caregiver can help with or how many hours you schedule.

Home care is generally billed hourly, and most agencies set a minimum visit length. Rates across the Dallas-Fort Worth area vary with the level of care, the schedule, and whether overnight or specialized dementia support is involved. For a fuller picture of what drives the number, see our breakdown of what home care costs in North Texas.

Long-Term Care Insurance

If your loved one bought a long-term care policy years ago, it may well cover non-medical home care. Many families forget the policy exists, or assume it only applies to a nursing home. Older policies were often facility-only, but most policies written in the last two decades include a home care benefit.

Pull the policy and look for four things: the benefit trigger (usually needing help with a set number of daily activities, or a documented cognitive impairment), the elimination period (a waiting period during which you pay out of pocket before benefits begin), the daily or monthly benefit cap, and any provider requirements such as care coming from a licensed agency.

VA Benefits for Veterans and Surviving Spouses

This is the option families most often miss entirely.

Aid and Attendance is an increase to the monthly VA pension for wartime veterans and surviving spouses who need help with daily activities. It is paid as cash, which means it can be applied toward home care without restriction on the provider. Eligibility depends on service dates, income, assets, and demonstrated need for assistance. The application takes time, so it is worth starting well before it becomes urgent.

Housebound benefits are a separate pension increase for veterans who are substantially confined to their home.

The VA Homemaker and Home Health Aide program and Veteran-Directed Care provide in-home support arranged through the VA health system. Veteran-Directed Care in particular gives the veteran a budget and real say over who provides the care.

Start with the social worker at your local VA medical center or with an accredited Veterans Service Officer. Neither charges for help with the application, and accredited representation matters here, because charging a fee to prepare a VA claim is not permitted.

Texas Medicaid and the STAR+PLUS Waiver

Texas Medicaid's main path to long-term home care is the STAR+PLUS Home and Community Based Services waiver, administered by Texas Health and Human Services. It is designed to help people who would otherwise need nursing facility care remain at home, and it can cover personal attendant services.

Two things families need to know. First, there are both financial and functional eligibility requirements: income and asset limits apply, and the person must meet a medical necessity standard for nursing facility level of care. Those figures are adjusted annually, so check the current thresholds with Texas HHS rather than relying on a number you read somewhere.

Second, there is an interest list, and it can be long. This is the part that catches families out. Getting on the interest list early, well before care becomes urgent, is one of the most useful things a Texas family can do. There is no cost to being on it and no downside to signing up and never needing it.

Call the Texas HHS interest list line or your local Area Agency on Aging to be added. If you are looking at Medicaid as your primary route, ask any agency you speak with, including us, whether they participate in the program before you plan around it.

Other Options Worth Checking

  • Area Agency on Aging. Collin County, Denton County, and the Dallas area each have one. They administer Older Americans Act programs that can provide limited respite and caregiver support, and they offer free benefits counseling that is genuinely useful even if nothing else comes of it.
  • Life insurance conversion. Some policies can be converted into a long-term care benefit account or sold through a life settlement. Worth exploring only with a fee-only advisor, since this market has uneven players in it.
  • Reverse mortgage. For a homeowner who intends to stay in the home, this can fund care. It carries real trade-offs and should not be entered into quickly.
  • Family cost-sharing. Siblings splitting a monthly amount is common, and it works best when the arrangement is written down at the start rather than renegotiated month to month.
  • Employer benefits and HSA funds. Some employers offer caregiver benefits or backup care. If care qualifies as a medical expense, HSA or FSA dollars may apply. Confirm with a tax professional.

What Most Families End Up Doing

In practice, most families in North Texas pay privately at first and layer in a benefit once it comes through. A common sequence looks like this: start care privately because the need is now, apply for VA benefits or file the long-term care insurance claim in parallel, and get on the STAR+PLUS interest list as insurance against a longer horizon.

The mistake to avoid is waiting to start care until a benefit is approved. Approvals take months, falls do not wait, and with long-term care insurance in particular, delaying care can actually delay the start of your benefits.

Getting a Real Number for Your Situation

Ranges only get you so far. What care actually costs depends on how many hours, which days, whether overnight coverage is needed, and what level of assistance is involved.
